## 1. Lean Creative Brief

### Working title
**There is vs. There are — How to Say What Exists**

### Learner profile
- Absolute beginners (A0), Vietnamese-speaking learners.
- Assume little or no prior confidence with English sentence construction.
- Explain concepts in Vietnamese; pronounce English examples clearly in standard American English.
- Voice direction carried forward from the project: Vietnamese male voice, Northern Vietnamese accent, calm but expressive; English examples in American English.

### Duration and format
- Target: 6–8 minutes, including explanation, guided examples, and five short practice questions.
- 16:9 whiteboard animation, accepted 2D visual identity.
- Main setting: a familiar classroom.
- Teaching method: discovery through visual challenges, followed by concise explicit explanation.

### Primary learning objective
By the end of the lesson, learners can choose and produce **There is** or **There are** in simple statements about one or multiple familiar countable objects.

### Supporting objectives
Learners can:
1. Distinguish singular from plural using object quantity.
2. Recognize the common plural spelling pattern of adding **-s** (book → books; chair → chairs).
3. Match **There is** with a singular countable noun phrase and **There are** with a plural countable noun phrase in the lesson's examples.
4. Include **a/an** in the singular examples shown.

### Success criteria
After viewing, the learner can:
- identify one object versus two or more objects;
- select “There is a book” rather than “There are a book”;
- select “There are three chairs” rather than “There is three chairs”;
- complete at least four of five basic recognition questions correctly (proposed target).

### Prerequisite knowledge
- Basic recognition of familiar classroom nouns: book, chair, desk, pen, bag.
- No assumption that learners already understand grammatical terminology; explain “singular” and “plural” in Vietnamese.

### Scope
**Included**
- One scene introducing singular and plural.
- Common **-s** plural pattern only.
- There is + a/an + singular countable noun.
- There are + number + plural countable noun.
- Positive statements only.
- Five two-choice questions, three-second thinking pause, answer reveal and brief feedback.

**Excluded**
- Negative forms: there isn't / there aren't.
- Questions: Is there…? / Are there…?
- Prepositions of place.
- Full a/an lesson or vowel-sound rules.
- -es, -ies and irregular plural forms.
- Uncountable nouns and more advanced existential constructions.

### Key misconceptions
- Using **are** with a singular noun phrase or **is** with a plural noun phrase.
- Forgetting **a/an** in the lesson's singular countable examples.
- Forgetting plural **-s** after a number greater than one.
- Treating the word “there” as a location word in this construction; explain it simply as part of the pattern used to say that something exists.

### Teaching and delivery constraints
- Explain in Vietnamese; keep on-screen English text short.
- Avoid excessive grammatical terminology.
- Use classroom objects and controlled object counts.
- Every scene will be a fresh, independent white canvas.
- When the user later approves the lesson, each scene must receive one complete, self-contained English image-generation prompt containing all visible text and illustration in the same image. No text-free image workflow or required manual text overlay.
- Preserve the accepted Style Bible and verbatim Typography Lock; no visible hand or pen in the static source image.

### Assumptions
- This is one standalone lesson in a broader English-learning sequence.
- Use the existing project voice preference unless the user changes it.
- Use five questions based on the user's established practice preference.

## 2. Detailed Curriculum / Lesson Plan

### 2.1 Lesson rationale
A0 learners need to notice quantity before memorizing the grammar pattern. The lesson therefore begins with a visual contrast between one and multiple objects, then maps that contrast onto **is** and **are**. A classroom provides familiar, countable objects and keeps vocabulary load manageable.

### 2.2 Concept sequence
1. Count the objects.
2. Name the noun as singular or plural.
3. Notice the common plural **-s** spelling pattern.
4. Use **There is** with one singular countable noun phrase.
5. Use **There are** with a plural countable noun phrase.
6. Compare both patterns and retrieve the correct one in short challenges.

### 2.3 Core rules
For the limited examples in this beginner lesson:

- **There is + a/an + singular countable noun**
  - There is a book.
  - There is an apple.
- **There are + number + plural countable noun**
  - There are two books.
  - There are three chairs.

Teaching simplification: “one thing → There is; two or more things → There are.” Immediately anchor this to the lesson's countable-noun examples. Do not present it as a universal rule for every noun type; uncountable nouns are outside scope.

### 2.4 Singular / plural foundation
- **one book** → **two books**
- **one chair** → **three chairs**
- Explain: singular means one; plural means more than one.
- Show **book → books** and **chair → chairs** as common examples of adding **-s**.
- Say “many common nouns add -s” rather than “all plural nouns add -s.”

### 2.5 Example progression
**Model 1 — singular**
- Visual: one book.
- Spoken model: “There is a book.”
- Focus: **is** + **a book**.

**Model 2 — plural**
- Visual: three books.
- Spoken model: “There are three books.”
- Focus: **are** + **three books**.

**Contrast**
- One chair: “There is a chair.”
- Two chairs: “There are two chairs.”
- Ask learners to count before choosing the sentence.

### 2.6 A/an treatment
Brief reminder only:
- “There is a book.”
- “There is an apple.”
Explain that the singular countable examples use **a/an** before the noun. Do not reteach the vowel/consonant-sound rule; that belongs to the separate Articles lesson.

### 2.7 Misconception correction
| Incorrect | Correct | Feedback focus |
|---|---|---|
| There are a book. | There is a book. | One singular noun phrase → is |
| There is three chairs. | There are three chairs. | Three objects → are |
| There are two book. | There are two books. | Plural noun form needs -s in this example |

### 2.9 Practice design and answer key
Each question presents a clear picture and two sentence choices. Give three seconds before revealing the answer. Feedback should name the quantity cue, not merely say “correct.”

1. Picture: one book.  
   A. There is a book.  
   B. There are a book.  
   **Answer: A**

2. Picture: three chairs.  
   A. There is three chairs.  
   B. There are three chairs.  
   **Answer: B**

3. Picture: one apple.  
   A. There is an apple.  
   B. There are an apple.  
   **Answer: A**

4. Picture: two bags.  
   A. There are two bags.  
   B. There is two bags.  
   **Answer: A**

5. Picture: four pens.  
   A. There is four pens.  
   B. There are four pens.  
   **Answer: B**

Avoid distractors that introduce unlearned grammar or unfamiliar vocabulary. Ensure the image count exactly matches the sentence.

### 2.10 Narration constraints
- Vietnamese explanations should use short sentences and one idea at a time.
- Pronounce each English model sentence clearly in American English.
- Avoid long grammar definitions such as “existential construction” in learner-facing narration.
- Use expressive contrast and curiosity, not an overly theatrical voice.
- Step 0 narration remains a draft until actual generated images are inspected and accepted.

### 2.11 High-level visual logic (not image prompts)
- A classroom observation hook.
- A clean one-versus-many comparison for singular/plural.
- One-object example paired with “There is”.
- Multiple-object example paired with “There are”.
- A balanced comparison module showing the two patterns.
- A guided example module including a brief a/an reminder.
- Five quiz states (question and answer reveal can be implemented in the exercise system; do not assume one static image can represent both states unless the scene brief explicitly requests it).
- A compact recap.

No image-generation prompts are included in this Stage B document.

## 3. Simulated Expert Panel Review

**Review status:** Draft review completed; no unresolved critical factual blocker identified within the stated scope.

### Lens 1 — Senior instructional designer / curriculum architect
**Score: 5/5**  
The prerequisite sequence is appropriate: quantity → singular/plural → noun form → There is/There are. The learning objective is observable and the scope is controlled.

**Finding:** The opening hook must not delay the actual learning. Keep it brief and make the learner count objects immediately.

**Required correction:** Keep the hook to approximately 35 seconds and explicitly transition from counting to the singular/plural distinction.

### Lens 2 — Beginner English teaching specialist
**Score: 5/5**  
The vocabulary set is concrete and familiar. Vietnamese explanation plus short English models is appropriate for A0.

**Finding:** Learners may not understand “singular” and “plural” without plain-language explanation.

**Required correction:** Define them first as “one” and “more than one,” then attach the English labels.

### Lens 3 — Grammar / linguistics / phonology reviewer
**Score: 4/5**  
The target examples are grammatical and the agreement contrast is clear.

**Finding:** “There is = one; There are = many” is a useful teaching shortcut but can be overgeneralized, especially when learners later encounter uncountable nouns. Also, not all plural nouns form by adding -s.

**Required correction:** Explicitly scope the rule to the lesson's countable-noun examples. Describe -s as a common pattern, not a universal rule. Keep uncountable nouns and irregular plurals out of this lesson.

### Lens 4 — Cognitive load / multimedia learning reviewer
**Score: 5/5**  
One visual context and a small vocabulary set reduce extraneous load. A side-by-side comparison supports discrimination.

**Finding:** Too many objects in the classroom image could distract from counting.

**Required correction:** Use only a small number of large, clearly separated objects per teaching beat. Do not add prepositions or decorative labels.

### Lens 5 — Assessment / misconceptions reviewer
**Score: 5/5**  
Five two-choice questions align with the primary objective. The three-second pause and answer feedback support retrieval.

**Finding:** A correct answer reveal should explain the quantity cue and preserve exact object counts.

**Required correction:** For every question, make the picture count and noun number agree exactly. Keep feedback to one short reason.

### Lens 6 — Visual explanation / storyboard reviewer
**Score: 5/5**  
The one-versus-many comparison is highly visual and naturally suited to whiteboard explanation.

**Finding:** Reusing a single classroom canvas across scenes would conflict with the accepted independent-scene rule.

**Required correction:** Treat each scene as a new white canvas, even when the classroom context repeats. Maintain modular, non-confusing object boundaries.

### Lens 7 — Technical production / timing reviewer
**Score: 4/5**  
The proposed 7:25 structure is plausible for the target duration. Quiz wait states are explicit.

**Finding:** Planning durations cannot be treated as exact TTS or SRT timings. A quiz question and answer reveal may be separate animation states, and the final narration must match accepted images.

**Required correction:** Keep timings provisional until actual audio/SRT is available. Generate no millisecond timestamps at this stage. Reconcile draft VO with accepted images before final VO freeze.

### Panel issue register
| ID | Severity | Issue | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| P-01 | MAJOR | “One = is / many = are” could be overgeneralized | Scope to countable noun examples; exclude uncountables |
| P-02 | MAJOR | “Add -s” could sound universal | Say “common pattern”; keep exceptions out of scope |
| P-03 | MINOR | Classroom visuals may become cluttered | Use few, large, clearly separated objects |
| P-04 | MINOR | Planned durations may be mistaken for exact timing | Mark provisional; use actual audio/SRT later |
| P-05 | MINOR | Quiz states may not map to one static visual | Plan question/reveal states explicitly in later production design |

### Dissent / trade-off
The beginner pedagogy lens favors the shortest possible “one vs. many” shortcut. The grammar reviewer cautions against presenting it as a universal rule. Lead recommendation: retain the memorable shortcut but immediately scope it to countable classroom objects and the sentence patterns taught here. This preserves beginner clarity without teaching a false universal rule.

**PASS.** The required corrections have been incorporated. No unresolved blocker or major issue remains within the limited lesson scope.
